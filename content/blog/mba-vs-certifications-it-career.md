## The Conversation Nobody Has in IT

You spend years building your certification stack. AWS Solutions Architect. PMP. CISSP. ITIL. Your LinkedIn profile looks impressive. Your skills are undeniable.

Then you watch someone with average technical skills — but sharp business instincts — walk into the CIO role you were targeting. And you wonder: what did they know that you didn't?

The answer, almost always, is **business**.

## What Harvard Business School Says About This

Harvard Business School embeds this belief into the core of its MBA curriculum. Their programme includes a required department — **Technology & Operations Management (TOM)** — that is not about writing code or configuring systems. It is about how technology decisions drive business outcomes at the enterprise level.

HBS also now includes **"Data Science & AI for Leaders"** as a required first-year course. Not "how to build AI models" — but how to *lead* organisations through AI transformation. That distinction matters enormously.

## The CIO Paradox

CIO.com — one of the most-read IT leadership publications globally — has been writing about this tension for years. Their editorial position is consistent:

<div style="margin: 2rem 0; padding: 1.5rem 2rem; border-left: 4px solid #10b981; background: #0f172a; border-radius: 0 12px 12px 0;">
<p style="color:#94a3b8; font-size:0.8rem; margin-bottom:0.5rem; text-transform:uppercase; letter-spacing:0.05em;">CIO.com</p>
<p style="color:#e2e8f0; font-size:1.05rem; font-style:italic; margin:0; line-height:1.7;">"The most valuable CIOs don't just understand technology — they know how to turn it into revenue, resilience and competitive advantage."</p>
</div>

This is the paradox every technical IT professional eventually confronts. You can be the best infrastructure engineer, the most certified cloud architect, the sharpest security analyst — and still be invisible to the board. Because boards do not speak in uptime percentages and ticket resolution rates. They speak in risk, revenue, market position, and strategic ROI.

An MBA teaches you that language.

## My Personal View: Technical Skills Are the Foundation, Not the Ceiling

Here is my honest opinion, based on years working in enterprise IT at SAP and managing stakeholders from Riyadh to global headquarters:

**If you have 5–8 years of solid IT experience, the marginal return on another certification is much lower than the return on business education.**

The reason is simple. At a certain point in your career, the question stops being *"can you do the technical work?"* — and starts being:

- Can you lead the organisation through a technology transformation?
- Can you present a business case to the CFO and get budget approved?
- Can you understand why the sales team's problems are actually your problems?
- Can you build a relationship with a CEO who has never written a line of code?

## MBA + Technical Background = The Rarest Profile in the Market

Here is something the business world has not solved well: most MBA graduates have weak technical foundations. And most senior IT professionals have weak business foundations.

The person who has **both** — genuine technical credibility and real business acumen — is genuinely rare and genuinely valuable. Organisations building digital transformation programmes, launching AI strategies, or modernising legacy infrastructure desperately need IT leaders who can operate in the boardroom *and* the server room.

In the Gulf specifically, Saudi Vision 2030 is creating enormous demand for this profile across NEOM, ARAMCO Digital, government digital transformation, and the banking sector. These projects require IT leaders who understand the national strategy, the business rationale, and the technical implementation simultaneously. An MBA from a world-class institution is a direct signal that you can operate at that level.

## Final Thought

Technical certifications are a ladder. An MBA is a different kind of floor — it changes the room you're standing in.

My view has not changed after more than a decade in enterprise IT: the professionals who reach the top of this industry are not always the most technically skilled people in the room. They are the people who understood the business, earned the trust of leadership, and could articulate why technology matters in terms that the entire organisation could rally behind.

If you have the technical foundation — and you do — the MBA is not a distraction from your IT career. It is the multiplier that makes everything you already know worth significantly more.
